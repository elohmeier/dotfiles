# Ideation And Sketching

Use this reference when exploring solutions, prototyping interactions, facilitating critiques, or designing before implementation details are known.

## Sketch To Think

Sketching is a design activity, not an art test. The goal is to externalize ideas quickly enough that they can be compared, criticized, combined, and discarded.

Good sketches are:

- Fast.
- Disposable.
- Ambiguous enough to invite discussion.
- Specific enough to test a decision.
- Annotated with intent, state, and assumptions.

## Diverge Before Converging

Generate multiple directions before refining one. Early refinement can make weak ideas look inevitable.

Useful prompts:

- Create ten different approaches to the same problem.
- Pick two or three and create ten variations.
- Force alternatives with different assumptions: novice vs expert, mobile vs desktop, high risk vs casual, assisted vs self-serve.
- Include one intentionally extreme option to expose constraints.

## Collect Triggers

Build a library of observed patterns, irritations, compelling designs, physical objects, screenshots, support tickets, and real-world workarounds. Use the collection to start conversations and avoid designing from a blank page.

Respect privacy when collecting examples from users, workplaces, or sensitive domains.

## Storyboards

Use storyboards when context, sequence, emotion, or environment matters.

Types:

- Sequential storyboard: shows the main path over time.
- Branching storyboard: shows decisions, alternatives, and recovery paths.
- Narrative storyboard: shows user context, motivation, interruption, and consequences.
- State transition diagram: shows system states and allowed transitions.

Annotate what the user knows, what the system knows, and what changes at each step.

## Prototyping Spectrum

- Paper sketch: concept and layout.
- Clickable prototype: navigation and flow.
- Wizard-of-Oz prototype: automation or intelligence simulated by a person.
- Video or animation: timing, motion, transition, and service choreography.
- Physical mockup: ergonomics, reach, posture, and tangible controls.

Choose the lowest-fidelity prototype that can answer the current question.

## Mental Model Checks

Before usability testing a full flow, show a static sketch or first screen and ask what people think it is, what they can do, and what they expect will happen. This exposes misleading visual cues and vocabulary early.

## Critique

Good critique is about fitness to goals, not taste.

Structure:

- Restate the user, goal, and constraints.
- Ask what assumption the design is testing.
- Identify what is working.
- Identify risks by principle and user impact.
- Suggest alternatives or next experiments.
- Capture decisions and unresolved questions.

Avoid design review arguments based only on personal preference; use user goals, evidence, and principles to decide what to test next.
