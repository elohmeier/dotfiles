# Research And Design Process

## Problem Framing

Do not accept the first stated problem as final. It may be a symptom, stakeholder preference, or implementation idea.

Reframe with:

- Who is affected?
- What are they trying to accomplish?
- What currently fails, costs time, creates risk, or causes anxiety?
- What constraints are real?
- What evidence would show the problem is solved?

## Double Diamond

Use divergent and convergent thinking twice:

- Discover: broaden understanding through observation, interviews, data, support tickets, analytics, and field context.
- Define: synthesize into a specific problem, audience, job, constraint set, and success metric.
- Develop: explore multiple solution directions and prototypes.
- Deliver: validate, refine, ship, measure, and learn.

Do not converge on a solution before understanding the work system and user goal.

## Human-Centered Design Loop

1. Observe and understand people in context.
2. Define needs, constraints, and failure modes.
3. Generate alternatives.
4. Prototype enough to test behavior.
5. Evaluate with representative tasks.
6. Iterate based on evidence.

Artifacts should serve learning. Use journey maps, service blueprints, wireframes, storyboards, prototypes, and task analyses only when they clarify decisions.

## Research Methods

Choose methods by question:

- Contextual inquiry: understand real work, environments, interruptions, tools, and informal workarounds.
- User interview: understand goals, language, expectations, attitudes, and past experiences.
- Usability test: observe whether people can complete realistic tasks with the design.
- Diary study: capture behavior over time or across contexts.
- Survey: quantify known patterns after qualitative discovery.
- Analytics: locate dropoffs, frequency, cohorts, timing, and behavioral signals.
- Support-ticket review: identify repeated confusion, missing states, and operational pain.

Triangulate. Self-report alone is not enough for interaction design decisions.

## Prototyping

Prototype the riskiest assumption first.

Use:

- Sketches for concept exploration.
- Clickable wireframes for navigation and labeling.
- Realistic data for comprehension and edge cases.
- Wizard-of-Oz prototypes for automation before implementation.
- Technical spikes only when feasibility is the biggest risk.

A prototype should have a question attached. If the question is unclear, the prototype is decoration.

## Usability Testing

Test representative tasks, not feature tours.

Good task prompts:

- Give a goal and context.
- Avoid naming the exact control to use.
- Include realistic data and constraints.
- Let users think aloud, but judge behavior more than opinion.

Observe:

- Where users hesitate.
- What they try first.
- What they misinterpret.
- Whether they notice feedback.
- Whether they recover from errors.
- What language they use for concepts.

## Success Criteria

Measure both outcome and experience:

- Task success.
- Time and effort.
- Error rate and recovery.
- Comprehension of state and next step.
- Confidence calibrated to actual result.
- Accessibility across input modes and assistive technology.
- Operational cost, support contacts, and downstream defects.
