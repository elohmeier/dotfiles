# Motivation And Persuasive Design

Use this reference when designing onboarding, activation, engagement, recommendations, progress systems, habit-forming loops, nudges, playful moments, or behavior-change flows. Use it with `business-and-ethics.md` for guardrails.

## Beyond Usability

Usability helps people complete tasks. Motivation helps people decide that the task is worth starting, continuing, and returning to. The design should support user intent, not manufacture dependence.

Good motivational design:

- Makes the next step feel achievable.
- Shows progress honestly.
- Reduces intimidation.
- Creates confidence through clear feedback.
- Uses tone, personality, and delight in proportion to context.
- Helps users express identity or preference when relevant.

## Aesthetics And Trust

Visual coherence, craft, and beauty influence perceived credibility and ease. Aesthetic quality should make structure clearer and the experience more humane.

Use aesthetics to support:

- Understandability.
- Legibility.
- Emotional fit.
- Confidence.
- Brand recognition when it helps orientation.

Avoid attractive surfaces that hide risk, make advertising look like content, or mask poor feedback.

## Small First Steps

People are more likely to continue when the first action is low effort and clearly useful.

Patterns:

- Start with a small, meaningful task.
- Ask for commitment after value is visible.
- Defer optional setup.
- Break compound requests into separate steps.
- Prefer clicks or selections over typing when precision is not needed.
- Let users skip personalization until they understand why it helps.

## Progress

Progress can motivate when it maps to real completion.

Use:

- Clear steps.
- Honest completion percentage.
- Endowed progress only when it reflects work genuinely done for the user.
- Saved progress and resume.
- Specific next action.

Do not inflate progress, hide remaining effort, or make completion feel mandatory when the user goal does not require it.

## Attention And Surprise

Attention is scarce. Use surprise, animation, humor, and novelty sparingly.

Good uses:

- Confirming success.
- Highlighting new value.
- Helping users recover from bland or stressful moments.
- Making optional exploration feel inviting.

Bad uses:

- Distracting from primary work.
- Interrupting serious tasks.
- Creating false urgency.
- Hiding ads, upsells, or consent choices.

## Defaults And Path Of Least Resistance

Defaults are powerful because many users accept them. They must reflect a responsible recommendation, not just a business preference.

Good defaults:

- Are safe for most users.
- Are easy to inspect and change.
- Preserve privacy and reversibility.
- Match the user's likely goal.
- Avoid unwanted public sharing, spending, subscription, deletion, or automation.

## Words And Framing

Language shapes decisions.

Use:

- Specific, plain labels.
- Benefit statements tied to user value.
- Consequence copy before risky action.
- Neutral choices for consent and refusal.

Avoid shame copy, confirmshaming, hidden negatives, manipulative anchors, and emotionally loaded wording for consent or purchase decisions.

## Behavioral Goal Review

Before using a persuasive pattern, answer:

- What behavior are we encouraging?
- What user goal does it serve?
- What business goal does it serve?
- What could go wrong if it works too well?
- Can the user decline, pause, undo, or leave?
- How will we detect harm, regret, or compulsive use?
